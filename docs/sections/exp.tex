\section{Experiments}\label{sec:exp}

To evaluate the impact of gradual magnitude-based pruning on OOD and adversarial robustness, we conducted experiments using the
ResNet20 architecture and the CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-10-C datasets \cite{resnet}. The datasets were presented in section \ref{subsec:cifar10c}.

The architecture of ResNet20 is presented in Table \ref{tab:resnet20}. $C(n)$ denotes 2D convolutional layers with $n$ output channels, $BN$ denotes
batch normalization layers, $GAP$ represents a Global Average Pooling layer, while $FC(n)$ denotes a fully connected layer with $n$ output units. 
To mitigate the vanishing gradient problem, the architecture includes residual connections in each residual block, which are denoted by square brackets.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{|c|}
    \hline
    C(16)-BN, [C(16)-BN, C(16)-BN]$\times3$, [C(32)-BN, C(32)-BN]$\times3$, \\ {}
    [C(64)-BN, C(64)-BN]$\times3$, GAP, FC(10) \\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\caption{Architecture of ResNet20}
\label{tab:resnet20}
\end{table}

For gradual pruning, we applied a custom strategy which was inspired by the Iteratively Reweighted $l1$-minimization problem \cite{boyd}. By updating 
the weights of the regularization term after each epoch, the regularization was more effective in pushing weights towards zero then common 
$l1$-regularization. The training process consisted of the following phases: a "warm-up" phase of 20 epochs without the sparsifying regularization; 
the "regularization" phase to drive weights towards zero while fitting the data; a pruning step where weights with magnitudes below a certain threshold
were set to zero; finally a "re-fitting" phase where the remaining weights could be updated to refit the data without the constant pressure of 
the regularization term. As the process was gradual, these three phases were repeated until a sparsity level of $95\%$ was reached. For evaluation purposes, 
models were saved at every $5\%$ step in sparsity. Note that the regularization term was defined on individual weights, not groups of weights.
The additional hyperparameters are listed in Table \ref{tab:training_params} in Appendix \ref{sec:appendix}.

\subsection{Sparsity-Robustness Tradeoff}

To distinguish the effect of pruning on test accuracy and robustness, I evaluated the pruned models on the original CIFAR-10 test set first.
Figure \ref{fig:test_acc} shows how accuracy changes during the compression process at different sparsity levels. To present the central tendency as well,
the mean and standard deviation of the test accuracy across 5 different random seeds are plotted. However, due to the random dynamics of the 
regularization process, the sparsity levels are not identical after the corresponding pruning steps across different runs. Therefore, statistics were computed
by assigning the models to predetermined sparsity anchor points and averaging the accuracies of the models which were mapped to the same anchor point.
Please note that at anchor point of $45\%$ sparsity, only one model was mapped across all runs, thus the standard deviation is zero.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{figs/test_acc_scaled.png}
    \centering
    \caption{Test accuracy of pruned versions of ResNet20 on the CIFAR-10 dataset.}
    \label{fig:test_acc}
\end{figure}

Although this plot does not quantify robustness, we can see how the model is able to maintain its performance below a sparsity level of $60\%$.
When pruned more extensively, the accuracy starts to degrade approximately linearly with the sparsity level.

Figure \ref{fig:cifar10c_acc} shows the test accuracies of the pruned models on the CIFAR-10-C dataset, averaged by corruption category across all severity level. 
The makeup of the categories is described in Appendix \ref{sec:appendix}. Note that the same anchor point aggregation methods was applied as well
to compute the statistics based on the different runs.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figs/corupted_acc_scaled.png}
    \centering
    \caption{Test accuracy of pruned versions of ResNet20 on the CIFAR-10-C dataset, averaged by corruption category across 5 severity levels.}
    \label{fig:cifar10c_acc}
\end{figure}
    
Not surprisingly, the performance of all models were degraded by at least $10\%$ regardless of the corruption category or the sparsity level.  
However, as the shape of curves corresponding to all four categories are similar to the one on the original test set -
resembling a nearly flat line until $60\%$ sparsity and then a linear decrease -, we can infer that
pruning does minimal harm to the robustness of the model against corruptions present in the CIFAR-10-C dataset. 
In other words, the pruned models are not more vulnerable to these corruptions than their dense counterpart.  

Regarding the outlier standard deviation of accuracies on noise corruptions, we may conclude that the robustness of the model
to high-frequency perturbations (i.e. gaussian noise, shot noise, etc.) is more sensitive to the the initial random initialization. However, 
this is not due to the pruning process, as the standard deviation appears to be similar across all sparsity levels. Therefore, the sensitivity of the model's performance
to high-frequency perturbations is likely an inherent property of the dataset and the training hyperparameters, rather than a consequence of pruning.

Apart from performance on corrupted images, we can evaluate the robustness of the pruned models by examining the distance between decision boundaries.
As mentioned in section \ref{subsec:ood&adv}, the norm of the minimal perturbation found by an adversarial attack is a good proxy for this distance.
The utilized attack is the Fast Adaptive Boundary attack, and the output of the attack will be referred to as FAB norm. Figure \ref{fig:fab_acc} visualizes the change in the norms as the sparsity level increases
during the pruning process. The same anchor point aggregation method was applied to compute the statistics based on the different runs. The FAB algorithm was
run for 10 consecutive steps.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figs/norm_sparsity.png}
    \centering
    \caption{Adversarial robustness of pruned ResNet20 on the CIFAR-10 dataset, measured by the norm of the minimal pertubation found by FAB attack.}
    \label{fig:fab_acc}
\end{figure}
    
Unlike the progression of test accuracies, the FAB norm starts to decrease earlier, after the $22\%$ sparsity anchor, and it decreases approximately
linearly afterwards. This indicates that the pruning process slowly but surely compresses the average margin to the decision boundary, thus 
making the model more vulnerable to small perturbations in its input. As the FAB attack is an iterative heuristic which finds
approximations of the minimal perturbations, the high standard deviation may be reduced by increasing the number of steps the FAB algorithm can take.

\subsection{Sensitivity Analysis}

To get more insight into how pruning affects OOD robustness, we utilized the Fourier sensitivity method described in section \ref{sec:fourier}.
Figure \ref{fig:sensitivity} presents two rows; the first row includes the Fourier heatmaps of the original dense model and five selected pruned models
at different sparsity levels. The second row visualizes how pruning changes the baseline sensitivity of the dense model by 
showing the difference between the heatmap of the latter and of the pruned models. Note that the heatmap does not show the error rate itself, but the normalized
difference between  the error rate on the perturbed test set and the error rate on the original, clean test set. Therefore, brighter colors correspond to higher
sensitivity to the corresponding Fourier basis perturbation. The reason behind plotting the normalized error difference instead of the error rate on the perturbed test sets
 was due to the continuous decrease of test accuracy of pruned models on the clean test set. 
 The aim of the plot was to visualize how the pruning process changes the sensitivity to distortions with specific frequencies and orientations, 
 neglecting the trivial overall sensitivity increase it causes. Normalizing them to be on the same scale also serves this purpose.
The heatmap was calculated based only on one pruning run and its corresponding models with a 
$L_2$ perturbation budget of $\epsilon=2.4$.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figs/fourier_diff_sensitivity_eps3*08.png}
    \centering
    \caption{Sensitivity heatmaps corresponding to pruned versions of ResNet20 on the CIFAR-10 dataset.}
    \label{fig:sensitivity}
\end{figure}

By comparing the heatmaps of the first row, we can see that until the sparsity level of $84\%$, the two bright blobs with a half-moon shape
remain relatively unchanged, although they appear to straighten out as sparsity increases. This indicates that the model does not lose its
robustness to low-frequency perturbations due to pruning, as the center of the heatmap remains dark. Moreover, we can interpret the bright blobs
loosing their curvature as pruning degrades the model's robust to horizontal pixel shifts, while maintaining its robustness to vertical pixel shifts.  
Based the fourth image, the pruned model with sparsity level of $84.53\%$ appears the most robust compared to the dense model. On the other hand, 
pruning beyond this level seems to cause a significant increase in sensitivity to all high-frequency perturbations corresponding to the bright
circle in the heatmap. The last figure in the second row also indicates this sudden rise of sensitivity in general. 

Apart from the pruned model at extreme sparsity, we might conclude that pruning preserved the overall shape of the Fourier sensitivity heatmap, 
thus maintaining the relative sensitivity to perturbations with different frequencies and orientations.
